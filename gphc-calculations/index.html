GPhC Calculations: Worked Examples and Practice Tips

Ten worked examples covering the calculation types candidates meet most, with the method shown so you can apply it to new numbers.

Last updated: 4 November 2026 · Written by a practising pharmacist

Official information first. Dates, format, permitted items and eligibility are set by the GPhC and can change. Use the GPhC registration assessment pages as your source of truth; see also our 2027 exam hub.

Method matters more than memorising answers. Write units at every step, estimate the answer first, and check it is sensible. Confirm which calculator, if any, is permitted with the GPhC.

1. Dose by body weight

A patient weighing 70 kg needs 15 mg/kg. The suspension is 250 mg in 5 mL. What volume is needed?

Dose = 15 mg/kg × 70 kg = 1,050 mg. Volume = 1,050 ÷ 250 × 5 = 21 mL.

2. Concentration

500 mg is dissolved in 250 mL. Express this as mg/mL and % w/v.

500 ÷ 250 = 2 mg/mL. 2 mg/mL = 200 mg per 100 mL = 0.2 g/100 mL = 0.2% w/v.

3. Percentage strength

A solution is 2.5% w/v. How many mg per mL, and how much drug is in 200 mL?

2.5% w/v = 2.5 g per 100 mL = 2,500 mg per 100 mL = 25 mg/mL. In 200 mL: 2.5 × 2 = 5 g.

4. Dilution

How do you make 500 mL of a 2% solution from a 10% stock? Use C1V1 = C2V2.

V1 = (2 × 500) ÷ 10 = 100 mL of stock. Add 400 mL of diluent.

5. Infusion rate over time

500 mg in 250 mL is infused over 4 hours. What is the rate in mL/hour, and in drops/minute with a 20 drops/mL set?

250 ÷ 4 = 62.5 mL/hour. Drops/min = 62.5 × 20 ÷ 60 = 20.83, so about 21 drops/minute.

6. Infusion by dose rate

A patient weighing 80 kg needs 5 micrograms/kg/minute. The syringe contains 250 mg in 50 mL (5 mg/mL). What is the pump rate in mL/hour?

Dose = 5 × 80 = 400 micrograms/min. Per hour = 400 × 60 = 24,000 micrograms = 24 mg/hour. Rate = 24 ÷ 5 = 4.8 mL/hour.

7. Unit conversions

Know these cold: 1 g = 1,000 mg; 1 mg = 1,000 micrograms; 1 L = 1,000 mL. So 0.25 mg = 250 micrograms, and 1.5 g = 1,500 mg. Always check whether an answer should be in micrograms or milligrams.

8. Millimoles

Sodium chloride 0.9% contains 9 g per litre. Relative molecular mass is about 58.5. How many mmol/L of sodium?

9 ÷ 58.5 = 0.1538 mol = about 154 mmol/L of sodium (and of chloride).

9. Body surface area dose

A patient is 170 cm and 70 kg. The dose is 50 mg/m2. Use the Mosteller formula: BSA = √(height in cm × weight in kg ÷ 3,600).

√(170 × 70 ÷ 3,600) = √3.306 = 1.82 m2. Dose = 50 × 1.82 = about 91 mg.

10. Renal function: Cockcroft–Gault

A 70-year-old man weighs 70 kg, with serum creatinine 100 micromol/L. Estimate creatinine clearance.

CrCl (mL/min) = [(140 − age) × weight (kg) × 1.23] ÷ serum creatinine (micromol/L), using 1.23 for men and 1.04 for women. = (70 × 70 × 1.23) ÷ 100 = about 60 mL/min.

Product information and local policy specify which renal measure (CrCl or eGFR) and which weight to use for each drug. Never use these examples to dose real patients.

Displacement values

A 500 mg powder vial has a displacement value of 0.3 mL. To get a final volume of 5 mL, add 5 − 0.3 = 4.7 mL of diluent.

Common calculation mistakes

  • Mixing micrograms and milligrams.
  • Forgetting that % w/v means g per 100 mL.
  • Using total daily dose when the question asks for one dose.
  • Not rounding sensibly, or rounding too early.
  • Skipping the sense check: does this answer make clinical sense?
